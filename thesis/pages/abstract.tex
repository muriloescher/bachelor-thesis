\chapter{\abstractname}

Morphological competence is essential for many \ac{NLP} systems, especially for languages with rich morphology. This thesis studies two standard tasks that make morphological knowledge measurable: \emph{morphological inflection} (generating an inflected form from a lemma and a bundle of features) and \emph{morphological analysis} (recovering lemma and \ac{MSD} from an observed word form). We additionally explore context-based analysis setups derived from \ac{UD}.

We present a unified experimental pipeline to train and evaluate diverse model families across multiple typologically diverse languages. Our language set spans high-resource languages (English, Italian, Portuguese) and lower-resource languages (Ancient Greek, Georgian, Lower Sorbian, Amharic, San Pedro Amuzgos). Our experiments compare established shared-task baselines (a non-neural baseline and a neural transducer) with modern sequence-to-sequence approaches based on fine-tuned ByT5. For analysis, we additionally consider inverse-direction ByT5 training on UniMorph data and context-based experiments derived from \ac{UD}, including a context-aware ByT5 variant and one-shot prompting baselines with different \ac{LLM} variants. Additionally, we propose a bidirectional setup that supports both tasks within a shared modeling framework. 

A central challenge in cross-dataset comparison is that UniMorph and \ac{UD} differ in file formats, annotation conventions, and tag inventories. To address this, we construct consistent train--development--test splits and a shared instance representation, and we map \ac{UD} features to a UniMorph-style inventory where official converters are available. We analyze how annotation and lemma conventions influence apparent outliers.

Across this curated sample, fine-tuned ByT5 models provide a strong and generally robust approach, and the proposed bidirectional setup performs competitively rather than being consistently worse than the corresponding unidirectional variants. We do not observe evidence that any single factor (language resourcedness, morphological richness, or pre-training coverage) consistently explains performance differences, highlighting the importance of dataset construction and annotation conventions when interpreting cross-language results.

All code and configuration files are organized to support full reproducibility of the reported results.
