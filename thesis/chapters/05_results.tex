\chapter{Results}\label{chapter:results}

\section{Morphological Inflection}

This section reports test-set performance on the morphological inflection task using the UniMorph-based datasets described in Chapter~\ref{chapter:datasets}. Each column corresponds to one language, identified by its ISO code (see Table~\ref{tab:languages-um}), plus an \texttt{avg} column that reports the mean across languages for each model. Results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results-inflection-matrix}, which reports both accuracy and mean Levenshtein distance.
\input{tables/results_inflection}

Accuracy measures exact-match correctness of the predicted inflected form, while the mean Levenshtein distance captures the average character-level edit distance between predictions and gold forms (lower is better). Throughout this chapter, boldface indicates the best model for each language within each metric; in all languages in Table~\ref{tab:results-inflection-matrix}, the same model is best under both accuracy and mean Levenshtein distance.

Overall, the best-performing models are split between the neural baseline and the ByT5 variants, suggesting that both strong task-specific architectures and large pretrained sequence-to-sequence models can be competitive depending on the language. Moreover, the bidirectional ByT5 variant is trained on a 50/50 mixture of forward and inverse UniMorph examples (Section~\ref{sec:bidirectional}), yet it remains competitive in the forward direction and even outperforms the neural baseline for several languages (e.g., \texttt{azg}, \texttt{eng}, \texttt{grc}, \texttt{ita}). A notable exception is Lower Sorbian (\texttt{dsb}), where the non-neural baseline achieves the best accuracy and the lowest mean Levenshtein distance, indicating that well-designed rule-based approaches can still match or outperform neural systems in some settings.

Overall, there is no single, consistent gap between high-resource and low-resource languages in these experiments: while Amuzgo (\texttt{azg}) confirms the expectation that a very low-resource setting can perform worse, this cannot be generalized to all lower-resource settings, as shown by the strong results for Lower Sorbian (\texttt{dsb}) and the decent results for Amharic (\texttt{amh}); more broadly, high-resource languages such as \texttt{eng}, \texttt{ita}, and \texttt{por} often yield strong absolute scores, but some languages remain difficult regardless of model family, suggesting that language- and dataset-specific properties (e.g., paradigm complexity and annotation conventions) dominate over a simple resource-based explanation.

With respect to ByT5 pre-training, ByT5 inherits its multilingual coverage from the mC4 corpus used for mT5 \parencite{xue-etal-2021-mt5} pretraining: high-resource and many medium-resource languages (including \texttt{eng}, \texttt{ita}, \texttt{por}, as well as \texttt{amh} and \texttt{kat}) are represented, whereas extremely low-resource or historical languages such as Lower Sorbian (\texttt{dsb}), Ancient Greek (\texttt{grc}), and Amuzgo variants (\texttt{azg}) are not.
In the results, this aligns with a general trend of strong performance for the clearly represented languages, but the remaining variation (including strong \texttt{dsb} performance for some models and persistent language-specific outliers) indicates that pre-training coverage alone does not determine difficulty.

\section{Morphological Analysis}

This section reports test-set performance on morphological analysis, evaluating (i) lemma prediction and (ii) prediction of the target \ac{MSD} (interpreted as a set of tags). Tables report per-language results (ISO codes; Table~\ref{tab:languages-um}) and an \texttt{avg} column giving the mean across the languages shown for each model and metric. Since the inverse/bidirectional ByT5 models are trained and tested on UniMorph, while the context-aware ByT5 model and the prompted LLM baselines use UD-derived data, results are reported separately for these two settings.

UniMorph-based results for the ByT5 inverse models are shown in Table~\ref{tab:results-analysis-um-matrix}.
\input{tables/results_analysis_um}

For UniMorph-based analysis, the model that achieves the best lemma accuracy for a given language is also best under lemma mean Levenshtein distance. Notably, the bidirectional inverse model (ByT5 bi-inv), which is trained on a 50/50 mix of forward and inverse examples (Section~\ref{sec:bidirectional}), achieves the best lemma results in exactly half of the languages (\texttt{azg}, \texttt{dsb}, \texttt{grc}, \texttt{kat}), indicating that splitting supervision across the two directions does not necessarily harm lemma prediction. For \ac{MSD} prediction, ByT5 inv yields the strongest results in most languages; the winner is consistent between \ac{MSD} accuracy and F1 in all but one case (\texttt{kat}), where the two metrics select different models. On average (\texttt{avg}), ByT5 inv performs better on all metrics except lemma mean Levenshtein, where ByT5 bi-inv yields the lower (better) distance.

\subsection{Context-Aware Analysis}

UD-based results for the context-based ByT5 model and the LLM baselines are shown in Table~\ref{tab:results-analysis-ud-context-matrix}.
\input{tables/results_analysis_ud_context}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:analysis-eval}, in the UD-based setting \ac{MSD} metrics are only available for languages whose UD-derived labels could be converted into the evaluation tag inventory; therefore, Amharic (\texttt{amh}) and Georgian (\texttt{kat}) have empty entries in the \ac{MSD} rows. In this case, the \texttt{avg} values for the \ac{MSD} metrics are computed over the subset of languages with available \ac{MSD} labels.

In the UD-based setting, the context-aware ByT5 model achieves the strongest performance for most languages and metrics (Table~\ref{tab:results-analysis-ud-context-matrix}), substantially outperforming the prompted LLM baselines. Ancient Greek is a notable exception: for \texttt{grc}, the LLM baselines outperform the context-aware model on lemma accuracy and, even more clearly, on \ac{MSD} accuracy and F1. At the same time, the lemma mean Levenshtein distances for \texttt{grc} remain high across all models, suggesting that the UD-derived Greek setting is particularly challenging in this configuration.

Two dataset-specific effects are worth noting when interpreting language-level outliers. First, in the prepared UniMorph subset used in this thesis, the Ancient Greek (\texttt{grc}) splits contain only nouns and adjectives, no verb instances, whereas the UD-derived analysis experiments focus solely on verbs. Consequently, Greek results are not directly comparable across the two data sources. This mismatch is a plausible contributing factor to why \texttt{grc} behaves differently in the UD-based tables, including the observation that the LLM baselines outperform the context-aware ByT5 model for Greek, despite the strong UniMorph-based results for ByT5 on \texttt{grc}. Second, Georgian exhibits a systematic mismatch in lemma conventions between UniMorph and UD: in the UniMorph Georgian data, verbs are grouped under a UniMorph-specific citation form (see also \parencite{guriel-etal-2022-morphological}), while UD lemmas for Georgian are typically based on the \textit{masdar} (verbal noun) form.\footnote{UD lemmatization convention is described in the \href{https://github.com/UniversalDependencies/UD_Georgian-GNC/blob/master/README.md}{UD\_Georgian-GNC README}.} This difference can substantially affect lemma-level scores and makes cross-source comparisons for Georgian particularly sensitive to annotation conventions.

Finally, the very low \ac{MSD} results for the prompted LLM baselines in \texttt{eng}, \texttt{ita}, and \texttt{por} are largely driven by systematic output errors rather than consistently producing entirely unrelated tags: common failure modes include generating strings that are superficially similar to valid tags but are not in the inventory (e.g., small character substitutions such as switching \texttt{V} to \texttt{T}, dropping the \texttt{V} prefix, or removing \texttt{V.} from \texttt{V.PTCP}), proposing plausible-sounding but non-existent tags (e.g., \texttt{PRET} for past), and producing incorrectly formatted outputs (e.g., emitting a literal \texttt{<tab>} token instead of the required tab separator, or wrapping tags in angle brackets). These near-miss and formatting issues are heavily penalized by exact-match and set-based evaluation, which helps explain the low \ac{MSD} accuracy and F1 despite occasional semantic proximity.
